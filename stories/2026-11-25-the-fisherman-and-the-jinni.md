# The Fisherman and the Jinni

Beside a wide blue sea, in a little house with a red roof, there lived a kind old fisherman. He had a white beard, a blue cap, and a striped shirt, and every morning he pushed his wooden boat out and cast his net.

He had a wife and three children, and he came back each day with fish for their supper.

One morning, he cast his net once and pulled up nothing but seaweed. He cast it twice and pulled up an old shoe. He cast it three times and pulled up a tangle of rope.

Then he cast it a fourth time — and oh! The net was heavy. He pulled and pulled, and up came a big brass jar, sealed tight with lead.

"A jar!" said the fisherman. "I wonder what is inside."

He pried at the seal until — pop! — it came off. Out of the jar poured a great cloud of smoke that rose and rose and twisted until it became a tall, tall jinni with a booming voice.

"At last!" roared the jinni. "I have been shut in that jar for a hundred years! Now I shall gobble you up!"

The fisherman's knees wobbled. He took a deep breath and spoke in his kindest voice.

"Wait, great jinni," he said. "Before you gobble me up, how did someone as big as you ever fit inside a jar as small as that?"

The jinni puffed out his chest. "Ha! Easy! Watch!"

With a great whoosh, he turned back into smoke and poured himself — swirl, swirl, swirl — right back into the jar.

The fisherman popped the stopper back on.

"Let me out!" cried the jinni. "Let me out, and I promise to be good!"

The fisherman smiled. "Promise me three times," he said. "Promise you will never gobble anyone, and that you will help me instead."

"I promise, I promise, I promise!" cried the jinni.

So the fisherman lifted the stopper, and out floated the jinni, bowing low. "Thank you, kind fisherman," he said. "Come with me. I know a secret pond where the fish shine like jewels."

The jinni led him to a round blue pond. In the water swam fish of red and white and blue and gold, flashing like little lamps.

The fisherman cast his net and caught the shining fish. He carried them to the market, and the people smiled at the prettiest fish they had ever seen. His wife cooked a warm supper, and his three children ate until their cheeks were round and rosy.

The jinni waved goodbye and floated away into the pink evening sky.

And the fisherman sailed home under a moon as round as a pearl, and tucked himself into bed, warm and safe and full of supper.

Sleep now, little fisherman.
