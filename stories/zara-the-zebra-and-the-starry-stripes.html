Zara the Zebra and the Starry Stripes

An original bedtime story for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.

On the wide, whispering savanna lived a young zebra named Zara. She had the shiniest black-and-white stripes in the whole herd, and she loved them very much.

But Zara had one question that no one could answer.

"Grandmother," she asked one evening, as the sun melted into the grass like warm honey, "why do I have stripes?"

Grandmother Zebra smiled her wrinkly smile. "Why don't you ask the night sky?" she said. "It knows everything."

So that night, Zara lay down in the soft grass and looked up. The sky was dark blue velvet, and the stars were scattered across it like tiny silver seeds.

Zara looked at the stars. Then she looked at her stripes. Then she looked at the stars again.

"Oh!" she whispered. "My stripes look like little pieces of the night sky!"

Just then, a tiny voice squeaked beside her. It was Pip, the field mouse, who was also looking at the stars.

"Your stripes are like roads," said Pip. "Roads for starlight to walk on."

Zara giggled. "Starlight roads!"

An old owl hooted from the acacia tree. "Hoo! Your stripes are like the bars of a lullaby, little zebra. Black note, white note, black note, white note — the night singing itself to sleep."

Zara closed her eyes and listened. And sure enough, in the soft night sounds — the crickets' chirr, the wind's shhhh, the owl's low hoo — she could almost hear a lullaby. Black note, white note. Dark, light. Dark, light.

"Grandmother was right," Zara whispered sleepily. "The night sky does know everything."

She curled her legs beneath her and rested her head on the cool grass. Above her, the stars twinkled — twinkle, twinkle — like a thousand tiny night-lights keeping watch.

And as Zara drifted off to sleep, she dreamed that her stripes lifted off her back and floated up into the sky, becoming new roads for the starlight to walk on — black note, white note — all the way to morning.

💛 Tonight's gentle lesson: Look closely at what makes you different — it might be a piece of the sky.
