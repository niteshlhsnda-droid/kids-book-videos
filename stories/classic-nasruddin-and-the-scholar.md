# Nasruddin and the Scholar

*A cheerful Nasruddin tale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.*

---

On the edge of a slow green river lived a boatman named Nasruddin. His boat was small and wooden, with a blue seat, a coil of rope in the corner, and oars that had rowed a thousand crossings.

One clear morning, a scholar in a long coat came down to the riverbank carrying a stack of books. He looked at the river, then at the boat, and he called out, "Good morning, boatman! Will you row me across the river?"

"Gladly," said Nasruddin. "Step in, and watch your footing. The wood is smooth from many feet."

The scholar settled himself among his books and opened the first one. Then he looked up over the top of the page. "Tell me, boatman," he said, "have you studied grammar — the rules of words?"

"No, sir," said Nasruddin with a smile. "I know the names of the river birds, and I know which reeds sing when the wind blows. But grammar never came aboard my boat."

"What a pity!" said the scholar. "Grammar is a fine thing. You have missed half of learning."

Nasruddin dipped his oars and rowed on. The water chuckled softly against the boat.

"And have you studied astronomy?" asked the scholar. "The stars, and their paths across the sky?"

"No, sir," said Nasruddin. "I know when the evening star comes out, and I steer home by it. But I never studied its name in a book."

"What a pity!" said the scholar again. "Astronomy is a fine thing."

"And poetry?" asked the scholar. "Surely you have studied poetry?"

"No, sir," said Nasruddin. "I hum the songs the river taught me. But I never learned poetry from a page."

The scholar gave a long sigh. "Then you have missed so much," he said, and he went back to his book.

Just then, there came a soft plip-plop, plip-plop. A thin trickle of water was curling in through a tiny crack in the boat. Nasruddin looked at it calmly. "A little leak," he said. "Nothing to fear. The shore is close, and the water here is shallow."

The scholar clutched his books. "A leak!" he cried. "What shall we do?"

Nasruddin smiled. "One question for you, sir," he said. "Have you learned to swim?"

"Swim?" said the scholar. "No! I never learned to swim!"

And then, right there in the middle of the river, they both burst out laughing.

"Then let us paddle together," said Nasruddin. "You hold your books high with one hand, and paddle with the other. I will steer."

So the scholar tucked his books under one arm, and together they paddled — splash, splash, splash — while Nasruddin steered the boat with his oar. The little leak kept trickling, but the shore kept coming closer, and soon the boat slid gently onto soft sand.

They stepped out onto the bank, dry and laughing. The scholar set his books down and shook Nasruddin's hand.

"Nasruddin," he said, "you know the river, and you know how to stay calm. I know books, and books are fine things. But today, your knowledge carried us both."

"And your books will carry you far too," said Nasruddin. "Every kind of knowledge matters — the kind in books, and the kind in hands that row."

The sun was low now, and the river glowed soft pink and gold. The river birds were flying home. The scholar picked up his books and walked up the bank, smiling. Nasruddin tied up his boat, and as the evening grew quiet, they both felt glad for the crossing they had shared.

The end. 🌙

---

*Moral: Every kind of knowledge matters — the kind in books, and the kind we learn by doing.*
