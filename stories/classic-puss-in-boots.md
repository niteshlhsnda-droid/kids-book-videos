# Puss in Boots

*A classic European folktale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.*

---

Once, in a small village where the sea air tasted of salt, there lived a miller — a man who ground wheat into flour — with three sons. When the miller grew old, he shared what he had between his three sons.

The eldest son inherited the flour mill itself, with its great wooden wheel creaking round and round. The middle son received the donkey, gray and shaggy, who clip-clopped down the lane with bags of flour on his back. And the youngest son? He received only the miller's cat. Just a soft, sleepy cat. What color do you think his eyes were?

The young man sighed. "What will I do with a cat?" But the cat sat up straight and said, "Don't worry. Just give me two things: a pair of boots, and a big bag. And trust me."

So the young man brought him boots and a bag. The cat slipped on the boots — left, right, how smart! — and off he went, proud as anything, with a swish of his tail.

First, the cat padded out to the grassy field, where tall grass tickled his whiskers and bees hummed over the flowers, and filled his bag with golden, sticky-sweet corn. Then he lay down very still, pretending to sleep, the bag open beside him. Along hopped a chubby rabbit, sniff, sniff, and POP! The cat tied the bag with a bow. The rabbit blinked. "But I was only sniffing!" he said. "Thank you very much," the cat purred.

Up to the palace he marched, boots tapping on the stone floor — tap, tap, tap! The guards stared, because cats do not usually knock. "Your Majesty," said the cat, bowing low, "my master sends you this fine rabbit." The king was so pleased that his booming laugh rang through the whole hall. "Tell your master thank you!" he said, and he gave the rabbit a cozy home in the palace garden, with all the carrots it could munch.

Two more times the cat brought gifts — a soft rabbit and a pretty wild bird. Each time he bowed low and said the same thing: "From my master, who thinks of you often." And with every visit, the king liked the kind young man more and more, even though they had never met.

Now, high above the village stood a tall stone castle, and in it lived an ogre — a big, grumbly storybook giant — who loved to brag about how great he was. The ogre gave a sleepy smile and said in a low, rumbly voice, "They say I can turn myself into anything at all."

The cat tilted his head. "Anything?" he asked sweetly. Now — what do you think the clever cat asked for? ... "Even... a tiny mouse?"

The ogre chuckled, low and rumbly. "Easy," he murmured. "Watch." And with a soft, sleepy sparkle, the great big ogre grew smaller... and smaller... until he was a tiny mouse. "Squeak," whispered the mouse in his tiniest voice, quite surprised at himself, and he tiptoed out the door into the quiet moonlit fields. He never came back.

With the castle empty, the young man made it his new home, and the king welcomed him to the palace at last. The king shook his hand warmly and asked him to stay for dinner — honey cakes and warm bread, and a golden saucer of warm milk for the cat, who licked his paw very politely and purred. They talked and laughed until the stars came out.

And the clever cat? The king gave him a grand job: guard of the big wheat room, where all the kingdom's wheat was kept. It is a very grand job indeed — and tonight, as you drift off to sleep, perhaps you can picture just how he spends his days.

The end. 🌙

---

*Moral: Being clever and kind matters more than being rich.*
