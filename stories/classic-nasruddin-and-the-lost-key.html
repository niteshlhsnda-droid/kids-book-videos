Nasruddin and the Lost Key

A Mullah Nasruddin tale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.

Nasruddin lived in a small, cozy house with blue shutters, at the end of a quiet lane.

Every evening, before his supper, he locked his little garden gate with his favourite key — a small brass key that shone like a tiny sun.

One evening, the wind blew hard, and the house was dark as a pocket. Nasruddin came in from the garden, rattling his key. "Supper time, Billo," he called to his cat.

Billo came pattering out of the dark hallway, her tail straight up like a flag. Nasruddin bent down to stroke her — and clink! The little brass key slipped from his hand, bounced on the floor, and rolled away into the dark.

"Billo, did you see where it went?" Nasruddin asked. Billo blinked her green eyes. Blink, blink. She had not.

Nasruddin patted the floor left and right. Nothing but dust. It was far too dark inside to see a thing.

"Well," said Nasruddin, "there is a much better idea." He walked out of his dark house, onto the lane, and stood under the tall streetlamp. Its golden circle of light lay bright and round on the road.

And there, in the middle of that bright circle, Nasruddin began to look for his key. He bent. He peered. He patted the ground.

Just then, his friend Amir came strolling down the lane, humming a tune.

"Good evening, Nasruddin!" said Amir. "What are you looking for?"

"My little brass key," said Nasruddin. "Help me look, please!"

"Of course," said Amir, and he knelt down beside him. They searched under the streetlamp for a long while — left, right, and all around its post.

No key.

Amir scratched his head. "Nasruddin," he asked, "did you lose your key here, under the lamp?"

"No," said Nasruddin. "I lost it inside my house."

Amir blinked. "Then… why are we looking out here?"

Nasruddin spread his hands wide and smiled. "Because the light is much better here!"

Amir stared at him. Then his shoulders started to shake. Then Nasruddin's shoulders started to shake. And then they were both laughing so hard that Billo came to the doorway to watch them, her tail curling with curiosity.

"Come on, you funny man," said Amir, wiping his eyes. "Let's go look where it really is." He picked up the little lantern from Nasruddin's doorstep and lit it. Its flame glowed warm and small, like a firefly.

Back inside the dark house they went. Billo padded softly behind them. The lantern light swung this way and that, painting wobbly pictures on the walls.

"Here!" said Amir at last. "Look!" And there, right under the door-mat where Nasruddin had stood to stroke Billo, lay the little brass key, winking in the lantern light.

Nasruddin picked it up and kissed it. "My tiny sun," he said. "I am sorry I left you in the dark."

That night, after supper, Nasruddin hung the key on a little hook by the door — where it would never roll away again. Billo curled up on the door-mat, purring like a tiny engine.

And Nasruddin fell asleep thinking what a funny thing he had done — looking where the light was easy, instead of where the key really was.

💛 Tonight's gentle lesson: Look for things where they really are — not just where it is easiest to look.
