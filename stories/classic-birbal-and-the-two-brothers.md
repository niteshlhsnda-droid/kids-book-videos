# Birbal and the Two Brothers

*A wise Akbar-Birbal tale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.*

---

In a little village, at the end of a dusty lane, stood a big old mango tree. Its branches spread wide like open arms, and in summer, it was covered in golden mangoes that smelled like sunshine.

The tree had belonged to a kind old farmer. When he went to rest among the stars, it passed to his two sons, Arjun and Kabir.

Arjun was tall and quick. Kabir was small and thoughtful. As little boys they had played under the mango tree, climbed its branches, and shared its mangoes all their lives.

But now that the tree was theirs, a quarrel began. "The tree is mine!" said Arjun. "I am the elder brother." "The tree is mine!" said Kabir. "I water it every day." "The mangoes are mine!" "No — mine!"

They argued morning and afternoon, so loudly that the sparrows flew out of the tree.

And while the brothers argued, the ripe mangoes hung on the branches, getting softer and softer, and nobody picked them at all.

One day, the villagers said, "This quarrel has gone on long enough. Let us take it to the king's court. Birbal will know what to do."

So Arjun and Kabir travelled to the palace and stood before King Akbar and his wise minister, Birbal.

"Your Majesty," said Arjun, "the mango tree is mine. I am the elder brother."

"Your Majesty," said Kabir, "the mango tree is mine. I water it every day."

The king looked at Birbal. "Birbal," he said, "what should we do with two brothers and one tree?"

Birbal smiled his calm, clever smile. "First," he said, "tell me about the tree."

So the brothers told him how their father had planted the tree when they were babies, how they had swung from its branches, and how they had sat in its shade eating mangoes until their hands were sticky and their faces were golden with juice.

Birbal listened to every word. "Come with me," he said.

He took the brothers back to the village, and they all stood under the big old mango tree. A mango fell with a soft thump onto the grass.

"Tell me," said Birbal, "can you cut this tree in half, and still have mangoes?"

Arjun frowned. "No," he said. "Half a tree would die."

"Can you cut its shade in half?" asked Birbal. "So each of you gets half the shade?"

Kabir shook his head. "No. Shade does not cut like that."

"Can you cut its memories in half?" asked Birbal softly. "Your father planting it. You two playing under it. All the summers you shared."

The brothers looked at each other. Their angry faces grew quiet.

"No," said Arjun.

"No," said Kabir.

"Then the tree cannot belong to one of you," said Birbal. "It belongs to both of you, just as it always did. And here is my plan."

He picked up the fallen mango and smiled.

"First," said Birbal, "you will take turns. One week, Arjun picks the ripe mangoes. The next week, Kabir picks them. Turn by turn, fair and fair."

"Second," said Birbal, "you will share every mango. Half for Arjun's family, half for Kabir's family. No mango is eaten until both families have their share."

"And third," said Birbal, smiling, "you will plant a new mango tree together — side by side, your hands in the same earth. So that one day, your children will have two trees to share."

Arjun looked at Kabir. Kabir looked at Arjun. And slowly, they both began to smile.

"Turn by turn," said Arjun.

"Share by share," said Kabir.

"And a new tree, together," they said at the same time.

So that very day, the brothers dug a hole in the soft earth beside the old tree, placed a little mango sapling in it, and patted the earth down together. Kabir brought water. Arjun held the sapling steady.

Then they picked the ripe mangoes — turn by turn, share by share — and carried them home. That evening, both families sat together under the old tree, eating sweet mangoes until their hands were sticky and their faces were golden with juice.

And from that day on, whenever anyone in the village quarrelled, the villagers would say, "Go and sit under the mango tree. Remember Arjun and Kabir."

The end. 🌙

---

*Moral: Sharing turns a quarrel into a feast — what is shared grows sweeter.*
