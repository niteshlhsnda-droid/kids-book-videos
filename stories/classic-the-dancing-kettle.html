The Dancing Kettle

A timeless Japanese folktale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.

Long, long ago, in a little village in Japan, there lived a kind old tea-master named Yoshi.

Yoshi loved to make tea. Every morning, he filled his old iron kettle with water, set it over the fire, and waited for it to sing — "Shhh-shhh-shhh!" — as the steam rose in soft curls.

One morning, Yoshi went to the market and bought a new kettle. It was round and black and shiny, with a little spout like a tiny nose.

"Now I can make tea for all my friends!" said Yoshi, smiling.

But that night, something very strange happened.

Yoshi was sitting by the fire when — CREAK! — the new kettle began to wobble. Then — POP! — two little legs popped out of its bottom! Then — BOING! — a furry tail popped out of its back!

"Oh my!" cried Yoshi. "My kettle has legs!"

The kettle hopped down from the fire — hop, hop, hop — and began to dance around the room! Round and round it twirled, its little legs going tap-tap-tap on the wooden floor.

Then the lid lifted, just a tiny bit — and two bright eyes peeked out!

"Peek-a-boo!" said a small, squeaky voice. "I am Poko, a little tanuki! I was hiding inside this kettle, and now I would like to dance!"

Yoshi laughed so hard that his sides ached. "Well, little Poko," he said, "if you can dance, then dance!"

And Poko danced! He twirled and hopped and spun. He tapped his little kettle feet — tap-tap-tap! — and the whole room seemed to dance with him.

The next day, Yoshi invited all the children of the village to see his dancing kettle.

"Look! Look!" cried the children, clapping their hands. "The kettle is dancing! The kettle is dancing!"

Poko danced for them all afternoon. He danced a slow dance. He danced a fast dance. He even danced a silly wobbly dance that made everyone giggle.

"Teach us! Teach us!" cried the children. So Poko taught them the tap-tap dance — tap with one foot, tap with the other, spin around, and bow! Soon all the children were tapping and spinning with him, and Yoshi hummed a happy tune as he poured the tea.

The children laughed and clapped, and Yoshi made sweet tea for everyone. The whole village was happy.

That evening, when the sun went down and the stars came out, Yoshi poured one last cup of tea.

"Thank you, little Poko," he said. "You have brought more joy to my house than a hundred kettles of tea."

Poko peeked out of the lid and smiled. "And thank you, dear Yoshi," he said. "You are the kindest tea-master in all of Japan."

And from that day on, whenever Yoshi made tea, the kettle would give a happy little wiggle — and somewhere inside, a small voice would sing, "La-la-laaa, time for tea!"

💛 Tonight's gentle lesson: A dancing friend is the best treasure of all.
