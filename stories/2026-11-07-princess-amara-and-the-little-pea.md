# Princess Amara and the Little Pea

Once, in a castle on a green hill, lived a young prince named Ravi. He wished to marry a real princess, so he travelled far and wide to find one. He met princesses who sang and princesses who danced, but something was never quite right, and he always came home alone.

One wild and stormy night, rain rattled the windows and lightning lit up the sky. Just as the castle clock struck nine, there came a knock at the great wooden door. The old king opened it — and there stood a girl, wet through, her hair dripping and her shoes covered in mud. "Please, may I come in?" she asked. "I am Princess Amara, and I have lost my way in the storm."

The queen mother welcomed her kindly. She gave Amara warm soup, dry clothes, and a seat by the fire. But as Amara dried her hair, the queen thought, "How can I know she is truly a princess?" She had an idea — a tiny green pea from the garden, no bigger than a fingernail.

While Amara sipped her soup, the queen slipped away to the tallest bed in the castle. She laid the little pea on the wooden bed frame. Then she piled mattress upon mattress — twenty soft mattresses in all — and on top of those, twenty feather quilts, fluffy as clouds. "Sleep well, dear," she said, patting the tall, soft tower.

Amara climbed the little steps up to the top of the bed and snuggled down under the quilts. But as the rain pattered on the roof, she tossed. She turned. Something small and hard pressed into her back. She tried her side, then her tummy. Still, the little hard lump poked and prodded her all night long.

In the morning, the queen asked, "Did you sleep well, dear?" Amara rubbed her eyes. "Oh, not at all," she said. "Something hard kept poking me all night. I am quite black and blue." The queen smiled a secret smile. Only a true princess could feel one little pea through twenty mattresses and twenty quilts.

Prince Ravi was delighted. He took Amara's hand, and they walked together in the garden as the sun dried the rain from the roses. "I could feel that pea all night," Amara laughed, "and I am glad it told the truth about me."

So Ravi and Amara were married in the spring, beneath the blossom trees, and the little green pea was kept in a glass box, where everyone could see it and smile. And that night, Amara slept in her own soft bed — no peas, no poking, just gentle rain on the roof.

Sleep now, little Amara.
