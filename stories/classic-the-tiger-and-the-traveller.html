The Tiger and the Traveller

A timeless Hitopadesha tale for ages 4–7. Reading time: about 4 minutes.

One bright morning, a traveller named Ravi set out on a long walk to visit his grandmother in the next village.

Ravi carried a small cloth bag with two warm rotis, a banana, and a little bottle of water. He wore a soft blue scarf, and he sang a happy walking song as he went.

"One step, two steps, down the dusty lane, past the mango trees in sun and rain."

The lane wound between green fields and tall grass. Butterflies fluttered over the marigolds. A cool breeze smelled of rain.

As Ravi came around a bend, he saw a big tiger sitting beside a shady banyan tree.

The tiger was old and slow, and his stripes were faded like an old blanket. In one paw he held up a shiny gold bracelet, and it glinted in the sunlight.

"Come here, traveller," called the tiger, in his smoothest, sweetest voice. "Come closer. Look at this beautiful gold bracelet. I found it in the river. I am old, and I cannot use it. I want to give it to you. It is yours, if you come and take it."

Ravi stopped. He stood still on the lane. He did not go closer.

The bracelet glittered and sparkled. It was very shiny. It was very tempting. But Ravi thought about his grandmother's words.

His grandmother always said, "Ravi, my child, if something looks too good to be true, stop and think. A stranger's gift is not always a gift."

Ravi looked at the tiger. He looked at the bracelet. He looked at the tiger's long, sharp claws resting on the grass.

"Thank you very much," said Ravi, politely, from right where he stood. "You are very kind. But I do not take gold from strangers. I have everything I need in my little bag."

The tiger's smile grew wider. "Oh, but it is free!" he said. "Just come a little closer. Just one step closer. The bracelet is yours. No one else will ever know."

Ravi shook his head, still smiling. "If it is free," he said, "then it must be too good to be true. And if I must step closer to a tiger to get it, then it is not a gift at all. I will keep walking, thank you."

"But — but —" said the tiger.

"No, thank you," said Ravi, kindly but firmly. "I wish you a good day."

And Ravi walked on, one step, two steps, down the dusty lane, singing his walking song again.

"One step, two steps, past the banyan tree, my grandmother's house is waiting for me."

Behind him, the tiger sat by his tree, holding his shiny bracelet, and nobody came to take it.

Soon Ravi reached his grandmother's little house at the edge of the next village. His grandmother was sitting in the doorway, shelling peas into a brass bowl.

"Ravi!" she cried, clapping her hands. "You are here! Come, come, sit beside me."

Ravi sat beside her and told her the whole story — the tiger, the shiny bracelet, and the polite no that kept him safe.

His grandmother hugged him tight. "You did exactly right, my child," she said. "A wise traveller knows that a glittering gift can hide a trap. You listened to your good sense, and you walked on."

Then she gave Ravi a bowl of warm rice and dal, and a sweet mango for afters. Ravi ate happily, and told her about the butterflies, and the breeze, and the marigolds along the lane.

That night, Ravi slept in his grandmother's little house, safe and cosy under a soft blanket, while the stars twinkled over the fields and the lane lay quiet in the moonlight.

💛 Tonight's gentle lesson: Beware of gifts that seem too good to be true — a polite no can keep you safe.
